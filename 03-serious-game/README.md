# Challenge 3 – Serious game

**In one sentence:** a fun game at the end of the session that checks whether participants have understood **everything** they learned.

## Our game: Put out the fire

- **Concept:** [`concept/game-concept.pdf`](concept/game-concept.pdf).
- **Playable version:** the same web app as the quiz ([`../quiz/`](../quiz/README.md#the-game-put-out-the-fire)). On the trainer screen, open the **Menu** and choose **Game**.
- **Slides:** minutes 26–41 of the [main deck](../slides/).

**In short:** the fire analogy of the slides becomes the rule of the game. The NGO has 4 rooms (work phones, social media, beneficiary data, office computers), each with a real incident. Each room is played in 4 phases, which are the 4 steps of the rule (Is it a fire? → Cut the fire → Call the firefighters → Fire report). Every phase has one right move (water) and two **trap cards** (petrol): the classic mistakes and the quiz misconceptions.

- **Mode:** one fire per room for the whole team, plus individual points and a leaderboard.
- **Timing (15'):** 1' rules (slides 20–21) + about 10' of play (16 phases × 25 s + results) + about 4' debrief.
- **Objective evidence:** rooms saved, % of right moves per objective (LO1–LO4 by phase, LO5 = trap cards avoided), the most chosen trap cards, and every person's answers (CSV).

## Purpose
- **Check and reinforce ALL the learning objectives** of the training together, not just one.
- **Use emotions and fun** (challenge, competition, time pressure, surprise) to help participants remember.
- **Show the trainer clearly** who has understood and which mistakes or misconceptions remain.

## When in the session
**26–41 min (15 minutes)**, right after the theory. The game lets participants use what they have just learned in a fun way, before the final case in [Challenge 4](../04-learning-by-doing/).

## Requirements
- Covers **all** learning objectives of Training 3.
- Works **online and onsite**, with no account creation or logins.
- Rules are understood in under a minute. Reusing a well-known game (cards, board game, escape game, role play, simulation…) is fine and saves explanation time.
- **The originality** is in how the game is combined with the incident response content, not in the rules. A "spot the phishing email" game does not count as original.
- Players must **apply** what they learned, not just remember it.
- Gives **objective evidence** of understanding (scores, choices made, steps in the right order…).

## Simple examples

**"Incident race" (card game)**
Each team gets an incident card (e.g. "Laptop stolen from the car"). They have 2 minutes to put the action cards in the right order (Stop, Disconnect, Report, Write down) and throw away the "wrong move" cards (e.g. "Pay the ransom", "Delete the evidence"). One point for each correct card. Fastest correct team wins.

**"Escape the locked office" (mini escape game)**
The NGO's computers are locked by a ransom message. To "escape", teams solve 4 short puzzles, one per step of the response. Each correct answer gives a digit of the final code.

**"Who do you call?" (role play)**
One player is a volunteer who has just found a problem, and the others play the coordinator, a colleague and a "hacker". The volunteer has to take the right steps while the "hacker" tries to push them into a wrong move ("Just pay, it's faster!").
